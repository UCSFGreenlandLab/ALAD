This post summarizes the 2026 International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation consensus definition of Acute Lung Allograft Dysfunction (ALAD), its validation in the LTOG cohort, the major etiologic categories, and what the molecular data are beginning to reveal.

2026 consensus definition

Acute Lung Allograft Dysfunction, or ALAD, is meant to give transplant teams a common language for an important clinical problem: a meaningful drop in lung function after transplant that may signal graft injury. While ALAD has been described in the literature for over 10 years, it has been inconsistently defined.

The 2026 ISHLT consensus definition describes ALAD as a decline in FEV1 of at least 10% compared with the patient’s maximum FEV1 from the prior six months.

ALAD is not a diagnosis by itself. It is a syndrome and a state of increased risk . When a patient meets ALAD criteria, the next step is to look for the reason: rejection, infection, airway problems, aspiration, medication toxicity, malignancy, or another process affecting the graft.

Why this matters: a shared definition makes it easier to compare management across centers, enroll patients into trials, and study whether early trajectories predict longer-term graft outcomes.

Validation in LTOG

The new definition was tested in the multicenter Lung Transplant Outcomes Group (LTOG) cohort, which included thousands of recipients and tens of thousands of spirometry measurements across many years of follow-up.

The validation work showed that a 10% threshold struck a useful balance between sensitivity and specificity. Thresholds in roughly the 9% to 15% range performed similarly, but 10% was a practical and well-supported choice.

The six-month baseline window also performed better than a shorter lookback window. It captured slower declines that might otherwise be missed, while keeping prognostic performance strong. In other words, the longer window helps identify the “slow-burn” cases that can still carry real risk.

Patients who met ALAD criteria had a markedly higher risk of graft failure, but ALAD was not always a one-way street. A substantial fraction recovered back toward baseline lung function, which is why the 90-day trajectory after the event is so informative.

Clinical takeaway: Outcomes from the 90 days after ALAD is identify help separate patients who recover from those who keep worsening.

ALAD etiologies

ALAD is heterogeneous. It is best thought of as a syndrome with multiple possible causes rather than a single disease entity.

  • Alloimmune ALAD: injury driven by the immune system, including acute cellular rejection and antibody-mediated rejection.
  • Non-alloimmune ALAD: infection, airway complications, aspiration, medication toxicity, malignancy, and other non-immune insults.
